Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify smishing attacks delivered through SMS text messages and recognize how they differ from email phishing in urgency and format
  • Recognize common smishing tactics including fake delivery notifications, bank alerts, prize notifications, and two-factor authentication scams
  • Understand why text messages feel more trustworthy than emails and how attackers exploit this perception to compromise devices and accounts

Introduction to Smishing

Smishing is phishing conducted through SMS (Short Message Service) text messages. Because text messages feel more personal and trustworthy than emails, smishing attacks often have higher success rates than traditional email phishing.

Key Concepts to Know

Understanding these core concepts will help you identify smishing attempts:

Delivery and Logistics Scams

Fake text messages claim that packages cannot be delivered and ask users to click a link to reschedule delivery or pay fees. These messages imitate legitimate shipping notifications.

Banking and Financial Alerts

Fraudulent SMS messages pretend to be from banks and warn of suspicious account activity. They may request verification of personal or financial information through a malicious link.

Prize and Raffle Notifications

Scam text messages claim that the recipient has won a prize, gift card, or raffle and must click a link to claim it within a limited time, creating false urgency.

Two-Factor Authentication Bypasses

Attackers send SMS messages that imitate legitimate authentication alerts or ask users to verify their identity, attempting to trick them into revealing actual authentication codes.

Shortened URLs and Link Obfuscation

Attackers may use shortened URLs to hide the true destination of a malicious link, making it difficult to know where the link leads before clicking it.

Real-World Examples

Learn from actual examples of smishing attacks and how to spot them.

Example 1: The Fake Delivery Notification

What happened: A remote worker received a text saying "Your package could not be delivered. Click here to reschedule: bit.ly/delivery123" from what appeared to be a delivery company.

Red flags: Shortened URL hiding the real destination, urgency to act, request to click an unfamiliar link, and an unexpected message.

Why it worked: The victim was expecting a delivery and the message felt legitimate because package notifications are common.

Example 2: The Bank Alert Scam

What happened: An employee received a text saying their bank account had unusual activity and asking them to verify their identity through a link.

Red flags: Suspicious link, generic greeting, request for verification, and pressure to act quickly.

Why it worked: People may trust messages that appear to come from their bank, especially when the message creates fear and urgency.

Example 3: The Prize Notification

What happened: Multiple people received identical texts claiming they had won a $500 Amazon gift card and needed to claim it within 24 hours.

Red flags: Unsolicited prize claim, limited-time offer, shortened URL, identical messages sent to many people, and no prior contest entry.

Why it worked: The promise of free money and fake time pressure made people act without thinking critically.

Example 4: The Two-Factor Authentication Code Trick

What happened: A freelancer received a text containing a two-factor authentication code and a link saying to click if they did not request the code.

Red flags: Suspicious security-related link and pressure to act through the message instead of checking the account directly.

Why it worked: The attacker attempted to make the message look like a legitimate security alert so the victim would trust the provided link.
