Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify vishing attacks conducted through phone calls and voice communication where attackers impersonate legitimate organizations or authority figures
  • Recognize how caller ID spoofing, voice manipulation, and technical jargon are used to establish false credibility and bypass skepticism
  • Understand the unique dangers of voice-based attacks where real-time pressure and emotional manipulation are used to extract sensitive information

Introduction to Vishing

Vishing is voice phishing conducted through phone calls. Attackers use phone calls to create personal interaction and real-time pressure that makes it harder for targets to verify the legitimacy of the call or think critically before complying with requests.

Key Concepts to Know

Understanding these core concepts will help you identify vishing attempts:

Caller ID Spoofing

Technology that allows attackers to make calls appear to come from legitimate numbers, such as your bank or company, by displaying a fake caller ID on the recipient's phone.

Real-Time Pressure and Urgency

Voice calls create immediate pressure to respond. Attackers use urgency, authority, and emotional manipulation to prevent targets from taking time to verify claims or think critically.

Technical Jargon and Authority

Attackers use technical language, internal terminology, and confident speaking patterns to establish credibility and authority, making targets less likely to question their legitimacy.

Information Extraction Techniques

Attackers ask seemingly innocent questions first to gather information, build rapport, and lower the target's guard before requesting sensitive information such as passwords or account details.

Emotional Manipulation

Vishing exploits emotions such as fear, urgency, trust, and embarrassment to bypass rational decision-making and pressure victims into acting quickly.

Real-World Examples

Learn from actual examples of vishing attacks and how to spot them.

Example 1: The Bank Verification Call

What happened: An employee received a call from a number matching their bank's official line claiming to be from the "Fraud Prevention Team" and warning of suspicious charges.

Red flags: Unsolicited call, urgent warning, request for sensitive information, caller ID that could be spoofed, and pressure to confirm details quickly.

Why it worked: The caller ID matched the bank, creating false legitimacy, while the fraud warning created urgency that reduced skepticism.

Example 2: The IT Support Call

What happened: A caller claiming to be an IT technician told an employee that a critical vulnerability had been found and requested remote access to install a security patch.

Red flags: Unsolicited technical support call, request for password credentials, use of technical jargon to establish authority, and claim of an emergency.

Why it worked: The attacker used confident technical language and an emergency scenario that made the employee hesitant to question the request.

Example 3: The IRS Impersonation

What happened: Someone called claiming to be from the IRS, warning of an audit and threatening legal action unless payment was made immediately.

Red flags: Threat of legal action, demand for immediate payment, unusual payment methods, high-pressure tone, and an unsolicited call.

Why it worked: Authority impersonation, legal threats, and artificial urgency created fear that interfered with rational decision-making.

Example 4: The Remote Access Trojan Attack

What happened: An attacker called claiming to be from a technical support company and warned that malware had been detected on the victim's computer.

Red flags: Unsolicited malware warning, request for remote access, technical jargon used to establish credibility, and urgency to act immediately.

Why it worked: The combination of authority impersonation, technical language, and malware scare tactics convinced the victim to grant remote access.
