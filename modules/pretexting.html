Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify pretexting attacks where attackers create false scenarios or impersonate authority figures to manipulate people into revealing sensitive information
  • Recognize how pretexting exploits trust, fear, and urgency to bypass normal security procedures and verification processes
  • Understand role-specific pretexting scenarios including IT support impersonation, payroll deception, and false emergency situations targeting remote workers

Introduction to Pretexting

Pretexting is a social engineering attack where the attacker creates a false scenario (pretext) to manipulate a target into revealing sensitive information or performing actions they shouldn't. Unlike phishing, pretexting is often conducted through direct communication like phone calls or instant messages.

Key Concepts to Know

Understanding these core concepts will help you identify pretexting attempts:

Authority Impersonation

Attackers impersonate authority figures like IT support, management, or government officials to pressure targets into complying with requests that seem legitimate due to the perceived power of the caller.

False Scenario Construction

Attackers craft elaborate false situations such as system outages, security investigations, and account suspensions using technical jargon and internal knowledge to convince targets the scenario is real.

Urgency and Fear Manipulation

Pretexting exploits urgency ("act immediately") and fear ("your account will be locked") to prevent targets from thinking critically or verifying the request through normal channels.

Trust Establishment

Attackers build rapport by asking harmless questions first, providing legitimate-sounding details, and using industry terminology to establish credibility before asking for sensitive information.

Verification Bypass Techniques

Attackers use pretexts to convince targets to skip normal verification procedures ("due to the emergency, we need to bypass the approval process") or to directly provide information instead of using secure channels.

Real-World Examples

Learn from actual examples of pretexting attacks and how to spot them.

Example 1: The Fake IT Support Call

What happened: An employee received a call from someone claiming to be from "IT Support" saying a critical security update needed to be installed immediately and asking for remote access to their computer.

Red flags: Urgent pressure to grant access, unsolicited technical support call, claims of emergency situation, request to bypass normal ticketing procedures.

Why it worked: The attacker used technical language, claimed internal knowledge, and created urgency that prevented the employee from verifying the call.

Example 2: The Payroll Department Deception

What happened: A freelancer received a call from someone claiming to be from "Payroll" asking to update their banking information due to "a system migration" and requesting account numbers over the phone.

Red flags: Request for sensitive financial information over phone, unsolicited payroll communication, urgency around account changes, no official notification prior.

Why it worked: The attacker used internal terminology, referenced real company systems, and claimed legitimacy through the false authority of the payroll department.

Example 3: The Security Investigation Pretext

What happened: An employee received a message claiming to be from "Security Team" investigating suspicious account activity and asking for their password to verify they weren't compromised.

Red flags: Request for password "for verification," urgent investigation language, bypass of normal verification procedures, communication outside official channels.

Why it worked: The pretext combined fear (account being compromised) with false legitimacy (security team investigation) to pressure quick compliance.

Example 4: The False Emergency Scenario

What happened: A manager received an urgent message claiming to be from the CEO saying "We have a data breach and need all employee contact information compiled immediately and sent securely. Do not tell anyone."

Red flags: Artificial urgency, emergency scenario with high stakes, request for mass data compilation, instruction to keep secret, unusual communication channel.

Why it worked: The false emergency scenario combined with authority impersonation and a secrecy instruction prevented the manager from verifying the request.
