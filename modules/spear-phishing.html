Introduction to Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a highly targeted form of phishing where attackers customize messages to a specific individual or organization using personal information. Unlike generic phishing, spear phishing feels personal and relevant, making it much more dangerous.

Key Concepts to Know

Understanding these core concepts will help you identify spear phishing attempts:

Personal Information Research

Attackers research their targets on LinkedIn, social media, and company websites to gather names, job titles, relationships, and work history to create convincing personalized messages.

Executive Impersonation

Cybercriminals pose as CEOs, managers, or authority figures within an organization and request urgent actions like wire transfers or credential submissions from employees who might not question authority.

Business Email Compromise (BEC)

Attackers compromise or spoof legitimate business email addresses and use internal knowledge to convince employees that requests are legitimate, often involving financial transactions.

Relationship Exploitation

Attackers identify professional relationships (clients, vendors, colleagues) and impersonate these trusted contacts to extract sensitive information or money through context-based requests.

Urgency and Secrecy Tactics

Spear phishing messages often include artificial urgency ("act immediately") and requests for secrecy ("don't tell anyone") to prevent targets from verifying the request with legitimate channels.

Real-World Examples

Learn from actual examples of spear phishing attacks and how to spot them.

Example 1: The CEO Impersonation

What happened: An accountant received an email from the "CEO" requesting an urgent wire transfer to pay a vendor invoice, with all company details matching their organization's practices.

Red flags: Urgent request for financial action, unusual communication channel, request for secrecy, pressure to bypass normal approval processes.

Why it worked: The attacker researched the company structure, used internal terminology, and the target hesitated to question the CEO's authority.

Example 2: The Client Impersonation

What happened: A freelancer received an email from their "longtime client" requesting an updated invoice template with banking information for a new project.

Red flags: Request for sensitive financial data, slightly different email format than usual, new request outside normal workflow.

Why it worked: The attacker found the client-freelancer relationship on LinkedIn and mimicked the client's professional communication style perfectly.

Example 3: The LinkedIn Research Scam

What happened: A job seeker received an email from "a recruiter" for a well-known tech company, offering a job interview based on their LinkedIn profile. The email included job details matching their skills.

Red flags: Unsolicited job offer with perfect match, request to download a "company handbook," link in email body rather than official portal.

Why it worked: The attacker used publicly available LinkedIn information to craft a convincing job offer email with a malicious download link.

Example 4: The Vendor Email Compromise

What happened: An employee received an email from their regular vendor requesting payment to a new bank account due to "corporate restructuring" with all correct internal order numbers and contact names.

Red flags: Unexpected change in payment procedures, unusual bank account information, minor details used to establish legitimacy.

Why it worked: The attacker compromised the vendor's email or spoofed it using internal information gathered from previous communications.
