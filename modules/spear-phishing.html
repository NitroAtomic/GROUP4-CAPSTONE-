Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify targeted spear phishing attacks that use personalized information to appear legitimate and increase success rates

  • Understand the unique dangers of spear phishing compared to generic phishing and how to spot subtle red flags in personalized communications

Introduction to Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a highly targeted form of phishing where attackers customize messages to a specific individual or organization using personal information. Unlike generic phishing, spear phishing feels personal and relevant, making it much more dangerous.

Key Concepts to Know

Understanding these core concepts will help you identify spear phishing attempts:

Personal Information Research

Attackers research their targets on LinkedIn, social media, and company websites to gather names, job titles, relationships, and work history to create convincing personalized messages.

Executive Impersonation

Cybercriminals pose as chief executives, managers, or authority figures within an organization and request urgent actions such as wire transfers or credential submissions.

Business Email Compromise

Attackers compromise or spoof legitimate business email addresses and use internal knowledge to convince employees that requests are legitimate, often involving financial transactions.

Relationship Exploitation

Attackers identify professional relationships such as clients, vendors, and colleagues, then impersonate these trusted contacts to extract sensitive information or money through context-based requests.

Urgency and Secrecy Tactics

Spear phishing messages often include artificial urgency such as "act immediately" and requests for secrecy such as "don't tell anyone" to prevent targets from verifying the request through legitimate channels.

Real-World Examples

Learn from actual examples of spear phishing attacks and how to spot them.

Example 1: The CEO Impersonation

What happened: An accountant received an email from the "CEO" requesting an urgent wire transfer to pay a vendor invoice, with company details matching the organization's normal practices.

Red flags: Urgent financial request, unusual communication channel, request for secrecy, and pressure to bypass normal approval processes.

Why it worked: The attacker researched the company structure, used internal terminology, and relied on the target's hesitation to question authority.

Example 2: The Client Impersonation

What happened: A freelancer received an email from their "longtime client" requesting an updated invoice template with banking information for a new project.

Red flags: Request for sensitive financial information, slightly different email format than usual, and a new request outside the normal workflow.

Why it worked: The attacker found the client-freelancer relationship through public information and mimicked the client's professional communication style.

Example 3: The LinkedIn Research Scam

What happened: A job seeker received an email from "a recruiter" for a well-known technology company, offering a job interview based on information from their LinkedIn profile.

Red flags: Unsolicited job offer, unusually perfect match to the target's profile, request to download a file, and a link outside the company's official recruitment portal.

Why it worked: The attacker used publicly available LinkedIn information to craft a convincing job opportunity.

Example 4: The Vendor Email Compromise

What happened: An employee received an email from a regular vendor requesting payment to a new bank account due to "corporate restructuring," with correct internal order numbers and contact names.

Red flags: Unexpected change in payment procedures, unusual bank account information, and reliance on internal details to establish legitimacy.

Why it worked: The attacker compromised or spoofed the vendor's email and used information gathered from previous business communications.
